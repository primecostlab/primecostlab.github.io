Why Is My Restaurant Busy but Not Profitable? Check Prime Cost First

Published 2026-10-08 · 4 min read

The short answer

A full room tells you about sales. It does not tell you what is left. The first place to look is prime cost, which is food, beverage and labor together. Restaurant365 (n.d., accessed 2026-10) puts full-service prime cost at 60% to 65% of sales. Above that, a packed Saturday can still lose money.

The formula in words

Cost of goods sold = beginning inventory + purchases − ending inventory.

Prime cost = cost of goods sold + total labor.

Prime cost % = prime cost ÷ total sales.

The inventory part matters. Purchases alone are not what you used. If you bought a lot of product late in the month and it is still on the shelf, it is not a cost yet.

Worked example (fictional restaurant)

This is a made-up full-service restaurant with full rooms on Friday and Saturday. The numbers are invented to show the math. They are not data from a real business.

| Line | Amount for the month | |---|---| | Total sales | $150,000 | | Beginning inventory | $18,000 | | Purchases | $49,500 | | Ending inventory | $17,500 | | Cost of goods sold | $50,000 | | Total labor (with taxes and benefits) | $55,000 | | Prime cost | $105,000 | | Left for everything else | $45,000 |

Now set the benchmark next to it. The top of the Restaurant365 range is 65% of $150,000, which is $97,500. This restaurant is at $105,000. It is $7,500 over the line.

Say rent, utilities, insurance, marketing, repairs and everything else total $44,000. The owner keeps $1,000 for the month, in full rooms.

ChowNow (2025) gives a net margin of 3% to 9% for full-service restaurants. On $150,000 of sales, that is $4,500 to $13,500. This restaurant is well below the bottom of that range.

Now picture the same month with prime cost at $97,500:

  • Left for everything else would be $52,500.
  • After the same $44,000 of other costs, the owner keeps $8,500.
  • That is inside the $4,500 to $13,500 range.

Same rooms, same sales, same rent. The $7,500 of prime cost is the whole difference.

Where would that $7,500 come from? Take labor first. At 34.2% of sales (the NRA 2025 median for profitable full-service operators), labor would be $51,300. That is $3,700 less than $55,000. The other $3,800 has to come from goods: portions, waste and pricing.

Why busy rooms still lose money

  1. Food cost is a guess. You do not count inventory at month end, so you use purchases as cost. Over a few months the guess drifts, and you find out when the bank balance does not match.
  2. The schedule does not flex. You staff for Saturday on Tuesday. Labor stays high while sales do not.
  3. Portions and waste. Heavy hands, spoiled product, remade dishes and comps never show up as a line. They show up as cost of goods sold going up.
  4. Prices stopped moving. Your suppliers raised prices three times. Your menu did not. Cost goes up and revenue stays flat.
  5. Costs outside prime. Rent and other fixed costs can be too high for your sales, even with a good prime cost. HigherMe (2021) gives 6% to 10% of sales for occupancy, citing Baker Tilly. On $150,000 that is $9,000 to $15,000. Check this third, after prime cost, because prime cost is the part you can change fastest.

What I do this week

  1. Count inventory tonight or on the next closing day. Write the number down with the date.
  2. Find last month's ending inventory, this month's purchases and the beginning inventory. Calculate cost of goods sold with the formula above.
  3. Add last month's total labor, including payroll taxes and benefits. Add it to cost of goods sold. Divide by total sales.
  4. Compare the result with 60% to 65% (Restaurant365, n.d., accessed 2026-10). If it is above 65%, find out which half pulled it up: goods or labor.
  5. Pick one fix for the high half. For goods, weigh the portions on your top three dishes. For labor, cut one position on your slowest shift.
  6. Do the same count at the end of next month, on the same day.

FAQ

Can a restaurant be busy and still lose money? Yes. Sales do not show cost. A restaurant with a prime cost above the 60% to 65% range (Restaurant365, n.d., accessed 2026-10) can have full rooms and a thin or negative margin.

What is a good profit margin for a restaurant? ChowNow (2025) reports a net margin of 3% to 9% for full-service restaurants. That is a wide range. Your own trend over several months tells you more than a single month.

How do I know if my prime cost is too high? Calculate it for a full month, with an inventory count at each end. If it is above 65% for a full-service restaurant, one of the two halves, goods or labor, is too high. Look at each one separately.
