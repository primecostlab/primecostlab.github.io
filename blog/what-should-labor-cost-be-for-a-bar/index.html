What Should Labor Cost Be for a Bar? (Benchmarks + How to Calculate)

Published 2026-10-06 · 6 min read

The benchmark: 25 to 35 percent of sales

Most profitable bars keep labor cost between 25 and 35 percent of total sales (Sculpture Hospitality, 2025). This range includes wages, salaries, payroll taxes, and benefits. A bar at the low end (25 percent) typically has higher revenue per labor hour: faster service, simpler menu, or higher check average. A bar at the high end (35 percent) may offer full table service, live music that requires extra staff, or operate in a market with high minimum wages.

For comparison, full-service restaurants run labor cost between 34.2 and 36.5 percent (National Restaurant Association, 2025), and cafes typically land between 30 and 35 percent (Zest Specialty Coffee Roasters, 2025). Bars can run leaner because beverage service requires fewer back-of-house staff than a full kitchen.

How to calculate labor cost percentage

Labor cost percentage = (Total labor cost ÷ Total sales) × 100

Total labor cost includes:

  • Hourly wages (bartenders, barbacks, servers, security, kitchen if you serve food)
  • Salaries (bar manager, assistant manager, yourself if you draw a salary)
  • Payroll taxes (FICA, federal and state unemployment, local taxes)
  • Benefits (health insurance, paid time off, workers' compensation, retirement contributions)

Total sales is your gross revenue for the period: all beverage sales, food sales if applicable, and any other revenue (cover charges, private events). Do not subtract discounts, comps, or credit card fees; use the top-line sales figure from your point-of-sale system.

Worked example: 60-seat neighborhood bar, one month

Sales:

  • Liquor sales: $28,000
  • Beer sales: $18,000
  • Wine sales: $6,000
  • Food sales (bar snacks, small plates): $10,000
  • Total sales: $62,000

Labor cost:

  • Bartenders (hourly): $12,000
  • Barbacks and servers (hourly): $4,500
  • Bar manager (salary): $4,000
  • Payroll taxes: $3,280
  • Workers' compensation and benefits: $1,720
  • Total labor cost: $25,500

Labor cost percentage: ($25,500 ÷ $62,000) × 100 → lands well above the 25 to 35 percent benchmark for bars (Sculpture Hospitality, 2025)

This bar is running above the 25 to 35 percent benchmark. Possible causes: overstaffing during slow shifts, high hourly wages in a competitive market, or low sales per labor hour.

What counts as labor cost

Always include:

  • Gross wages and salaries, before any deductions
  • Employer portion of payroll taxes (typically 7.65 percent FICA plus state and federal unemployment, around 10 to 12 percent total)
  • Health insurance premiums you pay
  • Workers' compensation insurance
  • Paid time off (vacation, sick leave)
  • Retirement contributions (401(k) match, if offered)

Do not include:

  • Owner's draw (if you are a sole proprietor or partner and do not pay yourself a W-2 salary)
  • Contract labor paid via 1099 (DJs, live bands, one-time event staff) — these typically go into operating expenses or entertainment, not labor cost
  • Tips retained by staff (tips are not an expense to you; they flow through your sales and payroll but do not add to your labor cost percentage)

If you pay yourself a salary on W-2, include it. If you take an owner's draw, do not include it in labor cost, but do account for it when calculating your true profit.

Why 25 to 35 percent matters

Labor cost and cost of goods sold (beverage cost, food cost if you serve food) together make up your prime cost. For bars, prime cost should be between 55 and 65 percent of sales (Backbar Academy, 2019). If labor cost is 30 percent and beverage cost is 20 percent, prime cost is 50 percent, leaving 50 percent to cover rent, utilities, insurance, marketing, loan payments, and profit.

A bar running 40 percent labor cost and 22 percent beverage cost has a 62 percent prime cost, which is at the high end of the acceptable range. If occupancy cost (rent, property tax, insurance on the building) is 6 to 10 percent (HigherMe, 2021) and other operating expenses run 10 to 15 percent, that leaves room for debt service, repairs, and net profit. Net profit margins for bars range from 7 to 15 percent (Small Business Trends, 2024), so a 62 percent prime cost is workable but tight.

A bar running 45 percent labor cost and 24 percent beverage cost has a 69 percent prime cost. Even with low occupancy cost and tight operating expenses, that leaves little room for a slow month, a broken cooler, or a loan payment.

When is labor cost too high?

Above 35 percent, investigate. Pull a report from your POS: sales per labor hour, by shift. Industry norms vary by concept, but a neighborhood bar should generate $80 to $120 in sales per labor hour. A high-volume nightclub may hit $150 or more. A wine bar with full table service may run closer to $60 to $80.

If your sales per labor hour are below $60, you are either overstaffed or underpriced. Compare your schedule to your cover count. If you scheduled three bartenders on a Tuesday night and served 40 customers, you had 13 customers per bartender. One bartender could have handled that shift, saving you 16 to 20 labor hours.

Check your hourly wages against the market. If you pay bartenders $18 per hour in a market where the going rate is $15, your labor cost will run 20 percent higher than a competitor paying market rate. You may choose to pay above market to retain experienced staff, but understand the trade-off: you need higher sales per labor hour or higher prices to maintain the same profit margin.

Review your benefits. Health insurance, workers' comp, and paid time off add 20 to 35 percent on top of gross wages. If you pay $20,000 in wages, your true labor cost is $24,000 to $27,000. Some operators do not offer benefits and accept higher turnover; others offer benefits and expect lower turnover and higher productivity. Neither is wrong, but the choice directly affects your labor cost percentage.

When is labor cost too low?

A labor cost percentage below 20 percent is rare and often unsustainable. Possible causes:

  • You are working 60 to 80 hours per week and not paying yourself a salary (your labor cost looks low on paper, but you are subsidizing it with your own unpaid time)
  • You are understaffed, leading to slow service, long wait times, and lost sales
  • You are in a very high-volume concept (nightclub, concert venue) where sales per labor hour exceed $150

If your labor cost is 18 percent and your customer reviews mention slow service or long waits, you are leaving money on the table. Hiring one more bartender on Friday and Saturday nights may raise your labor cost to 22 percent but increase sales by 10 to 15 percent, improving your net profit.

How to lower labor cost without cutting service

  1. Adjust your schedule to match traffic. Compare sales by day and by hour. If Monday generates $1,200 in sales and you schedule three staff for eight hours each (24 labor hours), your sales per labor hour are $50. Cut one person and sales per labor hour rise to $75, lowering labor cost percentage from 40 percent to 27 percent (assuming $15/hour wage plus 25 percent burden).
  2. Cross-train staff. A barback who can bartend during a rush, or a bartender who can run food, gives you flexibility to cover a call-out without adding a shift.
  3. Raise prices. If your labor cost is 32 percent and you raise prices by 5 percent across the board, your labor cost percentage drops to 30.5 percent (assuming labor dollars stay flat and sales rise by 5 percent). A $9 cocktail becomes $9.45. Test the increase on your top five sellers first.
  4. Track sales per labor hour weekly. Set a target (e.g., $100 per labor hour) and review it every Monday. If a shift falls below target two weeks in a row, adjust the schedule.
  5. Automate where possible. A self-pour beer wall, a kiosk for ordering, or a tablet-based POS that sends orders directly to the bar can reduce the need for a dedicated server or runner during slow shifts.
