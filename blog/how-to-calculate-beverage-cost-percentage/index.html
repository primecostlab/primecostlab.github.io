How to Calculate Beverage Cost Percentage (Formula + Example)

Published 2026-10-07 · 4 min read

What is beverage cost percentage

Beverage cost percentage measures how much you spend on alcohol and non-alcoholic drinks for every dollar of beverage sales. It tells you whether your bar or restaurant is pouring profitably or bleeding margin through over-pouring, theft, or pricing that is too low.

The lower your beverage cost percentage, the more margin you keep. A bar running a 20% pour cost keeps 80 cents of every beverage dollar to cover labor, rent, and profit. A bar at 30% keeps only 70 cents.

This metric applies to any concept that sells drinks: full-service restaurants, bars, pubs, cafes, and food trucks. The calculation is identical to food cost percentage, but the benchmarks differ because alcohol and coffee have different markup structures.

The beverage cost formula

Beverage cost percentage equals the cost of beverages sold divided by beverage sales, multiplied by 100.

Beverage Cost % = (Cost of Beverages Sold ÷ Beverage Sales) × 100

Cost of beverages sold is calculated using the inventory method:

Cost of Beverages Sold = Beginning Inventory + Purchases − Ending Inventory

Beginning inventory is the dollar value of all beverages on hand at the start of the period. Purchases are the invoices you paid during the period. Ending inventory is the dollar value of beverages on hand at the end of the period, counted and priced at the same cost you paid.

Most operators calculate beverage cost monthly, though weekly is better if you have the time to count inventory.

Worked example: bar beverage cost for one month

A neighborhood bar opens March with 4,200 dollars of liquor, beer, and wine in inventory. During March, the bar purchases 8,500 dollars of beverages. At the end of March, the owner counts inventory and values it at 3,800 dollars.

Beverage sales for March total 38,000 dollars.

Cost of Beverages Sold = 4,200 + 8,500 − 3,800 = 8,900

Beverage Cost % = (8,900 ÷ 38,000) × 100 = 23.4%

This bar's pour cost for March is 23.4%.

Industry benchmarks: what is a good beverage cost percentage

Beverage cost benchmarks vary by concept and by category of drink.

Restaurants with full beverage programs should target 18% to 24% of beverage sales, according to Backbar Academy (2019). MarginEdge (2026) observed the following averages across restaurant customers: liquor 14%, beer 21%, wine 28%.

Bars typically run total pour costs of 18% to 24%, with a median just above 20%, according to BevSpot (2016). BevSpot's customer data showed median pour costs by category: spirits 15%, beer 24%, wine 28%. Top-quartile bars held spirits at or below 14%, beer at or below 20%, and wine at or below 22%.

Cafes selling coffee and non-alcoholic beverages should target 20% to 25% of beverage sales, according to Zest Specialty Coffee Roasters (2025). This range applies to espresso drinks, brewed coffee, and other cafe beverages, not alcohol.

If your beverage cost percentage sits above these ranges, the usual causes are over-pouring, unrecorded waste or comps, theft, or menu prices that have not kept pace with supplier increases.

Why beverage cost matters more than you think

Beverage cost is one half of your prime cost, the single most important metric in restaurant and bar operations. Prime cost is the sum of cost of goods sold (food and beverage) and total labor cost, including benefits.

A bar with beverage cost at the low end of the benchmark range and labor cost well controlled will have more room for rent, utilities, marketing, debt service, and profit. A bar with beverage cost above benchmark and the same labor cost will have less room. That difference can be the gap between profit and loss.

Most profitable bars and restaurants hold prime cost between 55% and 65% of sales, according to Backbar Academy (2019). Controlling beverage cost is faster and easier than cutting labor, which is why operators track it weekly or monthly.

How to lower your beverage cost percentage

If your beverage cost is above benchmark, try these steps:

Count inventory consistently. Use the same method and the same person each period. Inconsistent counts produce false swings in cost.

Price your inventory at invoice cost, not retail. Ending inventory should reflect what you paid, not what you charge customers.

Review your pour sizes. A half-ounce over-pour on every cocktail can add points to your pour cost.

Check your menu prices. If supplier costs have risen but your menu has not, your beverage cost percentage will climb even if your operations are tight.

Track waste and comps separately. Record spills, broken bottles, and complimentary drinks so you know whether the problem is operational or promotional.

Compare by category. Spirits, beer, and wine have different benchmarks. A high overall beverage cost might be driven by one category, not all three.

Track your beverage cost every month

Beverage cost percentage is not a once-a-year number. It moves with supplier prices, portion discipline, and menu changes. Operators who calculate it monthly catch problems early, before they compound.
