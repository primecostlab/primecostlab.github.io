What Is a Normal Pour Cost for a Bar? (2026 Benchmarks)

Published 2026-10-07 · 5 min read

Why Pour Cost Matters More Than You Think

You run a full bar. Sales look good. But at the end of the month, your bank account does not match the register tape. The gap is often hiding in your pour cost.

Pour cost is the percentage of beverage sales spent on the alcohol itself. If you sold 10,000 dollars of drinks and spent 2,000 dollars on liquor, beer, and wine, your pour cost is 20 percent. It tells you whether your pricing, portioning, and inventory control are working.

Most bar owners guess. They assume 20 percent sounds right. But liquor, beer, and wine each have different cost structures. A bar pouring mostly craft beer will run a higher pour cost than one focused on well spirits. Knowing the normal range for each category helps you spot problems before they drain your profit.

Industry Benchmarks: What the Data Shows

BevSpot analyzed customer data in 2016 and published the most-cited pour cost benchmarks in the bar industry. The median bar sits at a total pour cost just above 20 percent. Top-performing bars (top 25 percent) keep it below 18 percent.

Breaking it down by category:

  • Liquor (spirits): median 15 percent; top 25 percent at or below 14 percent (BevSpot, 2016).
  • Beer: median 24 percent; top 25 percent at or below 20 percent (BevSpot, 2016).
  • Wine: median 28 percent; top 25 percent at or below 22 percent (BevSpot, 2016).

MarginEdge reported similar figures in 2026: liquor 14 percent, beer 21 percent, wine 28 percent (MarginEdge, 2026).

For total beverage programs (alcohol only), the industry average sits between 18 and 24 percent (Backbar Academy, 2019). Bars with strong inventory controls and consistent portioning land in the lower half of that range.

Why Each Category Runs a Different Pour Cost

Liquor has the lowest pour cost because spirits are sold by the ounce. A 750 mL bottle yields roughly 25 one-ounce pours. If you buy a bottle of well vodka for 12 dollars and sell each pour for 6 dollars, your pour cost is 8 percent (12 divided by 150). Premium and craft spirits push the cost higher, but the markup on liquor is still the best in the bar.

Beer costs more as a percentage because kegs and bottles have lower markup. A keg of craft beer might cost 180 dollars and yield 124 pints. At 6 dollars per pint, revenue is 744 dollars, and pour cost is 24 percent. Domestic light beer runs lower, but the category average sits in the low twenties.

Wine runs the highest pour cost because bottles are often sold by the glass at a lower markup than liquor. A 25-dollar bottle yields five glasses. At 9 dollars per glass, revenue is 45 dollars, and pour cost is 56 percent. By-the-bottle sales bring the category average down to the high twenties, but wine remains the most expensive category to pour.

How to Calculate Your Pour Cost

Use the inventory method. It works for one month at a time.

Formula:

Pour cost percent = (Beginning inventory + Purchases − Ending inventory) / Beverage sales

Example (liquor only):

  • Beginning inventory (liquor): 4,200 dollars
  • Purchases (liquor): 3,800 dollars
  • Ending inventory (liquor): 3,900 dollars
  • Liquor sales: 26,000 dollars

Cost of goods sold = 4,200 + 3,800 − 3,900 = 4,100 dollars

Pour cost = 4,100 / 26,000 = 15.8 percent

Run the same calculation for beer and wine separately. Then calculate total beverage cost using total beginning inventory, total purchases, total ending inventory, and total beverage sales.

What Drives Pour Cost Up

  1. Over-pouring. Free pours without a jigger add a quarter-ounce per drink. Over 100 drinks, that is 25 ounces of lost margin.
  2. Theft. Staff pouring free drinks or taking bottles home.
  3. Waste. Spills, broken bottles, drinks sent back.
  4. Pricing errors. Menu prices that have not kept up with supplier increases.
  5. No inventory count. If you do not count, you do not know what you lost.

What Good Operators Do

  • Count inventory weekly. Monthly is the minimum. Weekly catches problems faster.
  • Use measured pours. Jiggers or portion-control spouts eliminate guesswork.
  • Track by category. Liquor, beer, and wine each get their own line. You cannot fix what you do not measure separately.
  • Compare to benchmarks. If your liquor pour cost is 18 percent and the median is 15 percent, you have a 3-point gap to close. On 20,000 dollars of liquor sales per month, that is 600 dollars of lost margin.
  • Adjust prices annually. Supplier costs rise. Your menu should too.

Example: A 60-Seat Bar

Monthly beverage sales: 32,000 dollars (60 percent liquor, 30 percent beer, 10 percent wine).

  • Liquor sales: 19,200 dollars
  • Beer sales: 9,600 dollars
  • Wine sales: 3,200 dollars

Target pour costs (top quartile):

  • Liquor: 14 percent → target COGS 2,688 dollars
  • Beer: 20 percent → target COGS 1,920 dollars
  • Wine: 22 percent → target COGS 704 dollars

Total target beverage COGS: 5,312 dollars (16.6 percent of sales).

If actual COGS is 6,400 dollars (20 percent), the bar is losing 1,088 dollars per month, or 13,056 dollars per year.

How Pour Cost Fits Into Prime Cost

Pour cost is one piece of your prime cost. Prime cost is the sum of cost of goods sold (food and beverage) and labor (wages plus benefits). For bars, a healthy prime cost sits between 55 and 65 percent of sales (Backbar Academy, 2019).

If your beverage cost is 20 percent and your labor cost is 30 percent, your prime cost is 50 percent. That leaves 50 percent to cover rent, utilities, insurance, marketing, and profit. If pour cost creeps to 24 percent, prime cost rises to 54 percent, and your margin shrinks by 4 points.

Controlling pour cost protects your bottom line. A 2-point improvement in pour cost can double your net profit in a tight-margin business.
