How to Price a Menu Item Based on Food Cost

Published 2026-10-09 · 5 min read

Slug: price-menu-item-food-cost

The short answer

Divide the plate cost by the food cost percentage you want to hit. A plate that costs $4.20 to make, priced for a 30% food cost, sells for $14.00. For most restaurants, food cost should fall between 28% and 35% of total sales (MarginEdge, 2026). Cafes and food trucks are quoted at 25% to 35% of food sales (Zest Specialty Coffee Roasters, 2025).

That price is a starting point. You still check it against your neighbors and your labor. The steps below show how.

The formula, in words

Menu price = plate cost ÷ target food cost percentage.

Plate cost is everything on the plate that you pay for: ingredients, trim loss, sauces, garnish, the side, and the to-go box if the dish goes out the door.

Target food cost percentage is the share of the menu price you are willing to spend on food. Write 30% as 0.30 when you divide.

To check an existing price, flip it around:

Food cost percentage = plate cost ÷ menu price.

A worked example (fictional restaurant)

This is a made-up 60-seat restaurant. The numbers are for illustration only.

The kitchen sells a fried chicken sandwich at $12.00. Here is what one plate costs today:

| Item | Cost | |---|---| | Chicken thigh, trimmed | $1.80 | | Bun | $0.60 | | Pickles and slaw | $0.45 | | Sauce | $0.25 | | Fries portion | $0.70 | | Garnish and packaging | $0.40 | | Plate cost | $4.20 |

Divide $4.20 by $12.00 and you get 0.35. The food cost is 35%. That sits at the very top of the 28% to 35% range from MarginEdge (2026).

Six months ago the same plate cost $3.36. At $12.00 that was 28%, the bottom of the range. Nobody changed the menu. The price stayed put and the margin moved.

To get back to 30%, divide $4.20 by 0.30. The answer is $14.00.

Here is what that changes per sandwich:

  • At $12.00, you keep $7.80 after food cost.
  • At $14.00, you keep $9.80 after food cost.
  • Food cost drops from 35% to 30%.

Many owners round to a menu-friendly price such as $13.95. Just recheck the percentage after you round.

Do not price on food cost alone

Food cost is one half of prime cost. The other half is labor. A full-service restaurant prime cost is commonly quoted at 60% to 65% of sales (Restaurant365, undated, accessed 2026-10).

Median labor, including benefits, was 36.5% of sales for full-service restaurants in 2024 (National Restaurant Association, 2025). That is a median, not a target. Still, put a 30% food cost next to it and the two together land above the top of the 60% to 65% range.

So a 30% food target does not guarantee a healthy month. It only means the food side is inside the range. A dish that is labor-heavy, like hand-rolled pasta, may need a lower food cost target or a higher price. A dish that takes one pan and two minutes can carry a higher food cost.

Also check the market. Your price has to make sense to your guests. If the math says $14.00 and the place down the street charges $11, you have a different decision to make. You can cut portion cost, drop the item, or sell it on quality. The formula tells you what the dish needs. It does not tell you what the dish will fetch.

Why the math drifts after you price

A price set once is only right for one month. These are the most common reasons the real food cost ends up higher than the plan.

  1. Supplier prices rose and the menu did not. This is the example above. Recipe costs from last year are stale.
  2. Portions crept up. A heavier hand with the protein or the fries adds cents per plate. Across 200 plates a day, those cents are real money.
  3. Trim and yield were ignored. You costed the chicken at the invoice price per pound, not the price per usable pound.
  4. Small items were left out. Sauces, oil, garnish, bread on the table, and packaging add up and rarely make it into the recipe card.
  5. Waste and comps were not counted. Remakes, staff meals, and spoilage sit in your actual food cost. They are not in your recipe cost. The gap between the two is where this shows up.

The last point matters most. Recipe cost tells you what a plate should cost. Your monthly inventory count tells you what it actually cost. If the actual figure is well above the theoretical one, the price is not the problem. The gap is.

What I do this week

  1. Pull your top 5 sellers. Start with the dishes that sell the most, not the whole menu.
  2. Re-cost each plate with this month's invoices. Use the price per usable pound, not the old recipe card.
  3. Include the small stuff. Add sauce, oil, garnish, the side, and packaging.
  4. Compute the food cost percentage for each dish. Mark anything above your target.
  5. Check your real monthly food cost. Take beginning inventory, add purchases, subtract ending inventory, and divide by food sales. Compare it with the plate math.
  6. Decide on one move per flagged dish. Raise the price, trim the portion, or change the recipe. Write it down and date it.

FAQ

What is a good food cost percentage for a menu item? For most restaurants, 28% to 35% of sales is the quoted range (MarginEdge, 2026). Cafes are quoted at 25% to 35% of food sales (Zest, 2025). Individual dishes will land above and below. What matters is the blended number across the whole menu.

Should every dish have the same food cost percentage? No. A steak may run higher and a pasta lower. Price each dish on its own cost, then check that the mix of what you sell lands inside your target.

How often should I update menu prices? Whenever your top ingredients move enough to push a plate past your target. Re-costing your top sellers once a month is a practical minimum. [SOURCE REQUISE] for any industry standard on repricing frequency.
